Understanding matcha perception in Tunisia

Researching how urban consumers aged 20–40 interpret matcha, which associations and barriers shape perception, and how research can inform a clearer communication strategy.

The question

When a category is unfamiliar to part of its intended audience, communication cannot assume a shared understanding. This research project explored how urban Tunisian consumers aged 20–40 understand matcha and what meanings, associations or barriers may influence their response.

The approach

The research design combined several perspectives:

  • Focus groups to explore language, associations, initial reactions and consumer interpretation.
  • Survey research to examine selected perceptions across a broader set of respondents.
  • Competitor and market benchmarking across France, Morocco and the United Arab Emirates to compare category framing and communication choices.
  • Expert interviews to incorporate specialist perspectives and context.

Why the combination matters

Consumer language can reveal how people make sense of a product, while market comparisons can show how other businesses introduce and frame the category. Expert perspectives add context, but they do not replace consumer evidence. The analytical task is to distinguish repeated themes from isolated comments and be clear about what each method can support.

From research to communication strategy

The intended strategic application is to translate evidence into communication choices: how to explain the category, which perceived benefits to investigate, what uncertainties need to be addressed and which claims require proof. Research should inform which messages merit testing rather than assume that one creative angle will work for every audience.

The final interpretation must stay proportionate to actual findings, sample and limitations. A qualitative theme may explain how some participants think; a survey can help estimate how often a response appears within its sample. Neither should be presented as universal without appropriate evidence.

The strategic lesson

When a product's meaning is not self-evident, effective communication starts with understanding the audience's existing mental model. Research can identify where explanation, relevance or trust may need work before a brand invests in more content or media.

Publication note. This case describes the research objective and methods. It does not publish unverified findings, market-size claims or performance outcomes.
