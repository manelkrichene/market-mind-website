Market research should change a decision, not just describe a market.

A decision-first framework for turning market research into a practical brief: the decision, evidence, interpretation, options and next test.

MARKET MIND FIELD NOTES7 MIN READOCTOBER 2026

Market research can produce a lot of information and still leave a team unsure what to do. The problem is often not insufficient data. It is that the research was never anchored to a decision the business needs to make.

A report is useful when it helps someone choose: which audience to prioritize, what to validate, where to compete, what to stop doing, or which risk deserves attention first. Without that connection, findings can become an archive rather than an input to strategy.

Start with the decision, not the method

Before selecting interviews, surveys, desk research or competitor analysis, write down the decision the work is meant to inform. Make it specific enough to guide scope.

Weak brief: “We need to understand the market.”

Stronger brief: “We need to decide which of two customer segments to prioritize for our first commercial offer, and what evidence would change that choice.”

The second brief reveals which information is relevant, which assumptions need testing and what the final deliverable should help the team decide. It also gives research a natural stopping point: enough evidence to improve the decision, not an endless search for perfect certainty.

Separate observation from interpretation

Keep three layers distinct:

  • Observation: what the evidence directly shows, such as a competitor's stated offer, a repeated interview theme or a survey response.
  • Interpretation: what the observation may mean in context. This is where patterns, tensions and explanations are considered.
  • Implication: what the business could do differently because of that interpretation.

When these layers are blended, teams can mistake a plausible explanation for a proven fact. Marking them separately makes the reasoning easier to challenge and the recommendation more credible.

Triangulate instead of searching for one perfect source

Different methods answer different questions. Desk research can establish context. Competitor analysis can reveal category framing. Interviews can uncover language, perceived trade-offs and hidden objections. Surveys can help assess how widespread a pattern may be within a defined sample. Behavioural or sales data can show what people do in a particular setting.

No single method automatically replaces the others. Compare sources when possible and record where they agree, where they conflict and what remains unknown. If a research design cannot support a broad conclusion, say so explicitly.

Build a decision brief, not simply a report

  1. The decision: what must be decided, by whom and by when.
  2. The evidence: the most relevant observations and their sources.
  3. The interpretation: patterns and explanations best supported by the evidence.
  4. The options: realistic paths forward, including trade-offs.
  5. The recommendation: the preferred path and why it fits.
  6. The next test: what to measure or learn after acting.

Strategy should not imply that uncertainty has disappeared. It should make uncertainty visible and define how the team will learn as it moves.

Know what the evidence cannot tell you

Research can reveal blind spots and narrow the range of plausible choices. It cannot guarantee demand, eliminate execution risk or predict every competitor response. Conclusions depend on the quality of the evidence, sample, context and assumptions.

Make confidence levels visible. Separate findings from hypotheses. Identify what would change the recommendation. This is not a weakness; it is a sign that the work is designed to support real decisions.

A useful final question

Before delivering a market study, ask: “What should the reader decide, stop, start or test because of this work?” If the answer is unclear, the study may need a sharper question rather than another chart.
