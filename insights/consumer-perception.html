Consumer insight

Consumer perception: the gap between a product and what it means.

Understand consumer perception through category knowledge, associations, trust, perceived value, context and the difference between stated preference and behaviour.

MARKET MIND FIELD NOTES6 MIN READOCTOBER 2026

A product can be objectively well made and still be misunderstood. A category can be familiar to specialists and unfamiliar to intended buyers. A message can be clear to a marketing team and mean something different to the audience.

Consumer research needs to investigate not only what a product is, but what it represents in a person's mind, what people believe it will do, and which doubts or associations influence their willingness to consider it.

Perception is built from more than features

People interpret a product through experience, prior knowledge, social meaning, price cues, packaging, recommendations and the language used to describe it. These signals interact. A premium price may communicate quality to one buyer and unnecessary risk to another. A new ingredient may signal novelty to one group and unfamiliarity to another.

Investigate at least five dimensions:

  • Category understanding: what do people think the product is and how do they distinguish it from alternatives?
  • Associations: which words, situations, people or outcomes connect with it?
  • Perceived benefit: what job or problem do they believe it can help with?
  • Trust and risk: what makes a claim believable, confusing or difficult to evaluate?
  • Context: when, where and with whom would the product feel relevant?

Do not confuse what people say with what they will do

Stated preference is useful, but it is not a guaranteed forecast of behaviour. Participants may give socially desirable answers, react to a hypothetical scenario or express interest without intending to pay.

Exploratory interviews can uncover language, meaning and objections. Surveys can measure selected attitudes in a defined sample. Concept tests can compare responses to different propositions. Behavioural evidence, pre-orders or carefully designed purchase tests can provide a more direct signal of action where feasible and appropriate. Each method has limits: a small qualitative sample is not automatically representative, and a survey response does not prove purchase intent.

Ask questions that reveal the mental model

  • What comes to mind when you hear this category?
  • What would you compare it with, and why?
  • What would make you trust or doubt this claim?
  • When would this product be useful to you? When would it not?
  • What would you need to know before considering a purchase?
  • What would make the price feel justified, or unjustified?

Use follow-up questions to understand the reasoning behind answers. Avoid pushing participants toward the explanation the team hopes to hear.

Translate insight into communication choices

Research becomes useful when it changes what a brand chooses to explain, demonstrate or leave out. If people do not understand the category, the first task may be education. If they understand it but distrust a claim, evidence and transparency may matter more. If the benefit is relevant but hard to picture, communication may need a concrete use case rather than another abstract promise.

These are hypotheses to test, not universal rules. Link each identified barrier to a communication response and observe whether it improves understanding or consideration.

Make the interpretation explicit

A strong consumer insight does not merely repeat a quote. It connects a meaningful pattern to a decision. Record the evidence behind it, the audience in which it appeared, alternative explanations and what to test next.

The goal is not to make consumers fit the strategy. It is to make the strategy fit what the evidence reveals about people.

Consumer understanding is most valuable when it changes product framing, value proposition, proof points, audience prioritization or the way a business tests demand.
