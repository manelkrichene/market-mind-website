Go-to-market strategy

Before you launch, design a test that can prove you wrong.

A five-step pre-launch validation approach for physical product founders: assumptions, buyer evidence, proposition tests, purchase signals and decision thresholds.

MARKET MIND FIELD NOTES8 MIN READOCTOBER 2026

Launching a physical product can make an idea feel real. It can also convert an untested assumption into inventory, packaging, storage costs and a commitment that becomes difficult to reverse.

Pre-launch research is not about proving an idea is brilliant. It is about finding which assumptions are most likely to break the plan before the cost of learning becomes too high.

Gate 1 — Make assumptions visible

Write down the beliefs that must be true for the launch to work: the problem, audience, purchase frequency, price, channel access, margin, lead time or repeat purchase. Separate facts from estimates and assumptions. Rank assumptions by uncertainty and the cost of being wrong.

Useful prompt: If this assumption is wrong, what becomes expensive, slow or impossible? Start with the highest-consequence uncertainties.

Gate 2 — Understand the real alternative

Customers compare a product with direct competitors, substitutes, familiar routines and sometimes no purchase at all. Understand current behaviour, why it persists and what would make switching worthwhile. A new product must earn a place in a real decision, not simply attract interest in a presentation.

Gate 3 — Test the proposition, not just the packaging

Attractive visuals can mask a weak proposition. Test whether people understand the offer, recognize the intended benefit, trust the evidence and can explain why it matters to them. Compare a small number of differentiated propositions and keep the audience and context comparable.

Gate 4 — Seek stronger demand signals

Use a sequence of evidence appropriate to the cost and risk. Interviews can reveal needs. A landing page can test whether a proposition earns attention. A qualified waitlist can reveal who takes a next step. Pre-orders or paid pilots may provide stronger signals where legally, operationally and ethically feasible.

Be precise about what each signal proves. A click is not a sale; a waitlist is not proof of repeat demand. Keep interpretation proportional to the evidence.

Gate 5 — Decide in advance what happens next

Define thresholds before running the test. What would justify proceeding? What triggers another test, a change in proposition or a pause? What is the maximum acceptable cost of learning? Predefined thresholds reduce the temptation to reinterpret weak results after the fact.

Include commercial reality

Demand is only one part of a viable product launch. Model unit economics, minimum order quantities, lead times, payment terms, shipping, returns, quality control and cash tied up in inventory. Use conservative scenarios and identify break-even conditions. A market can be interested while the current model remains unworkable.

Launch with evidence, not certainty

No test removes every risk. A disciplined process narrows uncertainty, clarifies the next investment and gives the founder a reasoned basis for proceeding, adapting or stopping. That is the thinking behind Launch Before You Stock.
