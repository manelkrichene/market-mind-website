Competitive intelligence

Competitive intelligence is about what the pattern means next.

A practical system for competitor intelligence: define the decision, observe comparable signals, map strategic patterns and identify credible choices.

MARKET MIND FIELD NOTES8 MIN READOCTOBER 2026

Competitive intelligence is often reduced to screenshots, a spreadsheet of prices and a list of social posts. These observations may be useful, but collecting them does not automatically create an advantage.

The value comes from connecting visible signals to a strategic question: which customer is underserved, which claim is becoming the category norm, where is the market getting more crowded, and which trade-off could create a distinct position?

Start with the decision

Different decisions require different competitor sets. A pricing decision may require direct alternatives and substitutes. A positioning decision may require brands competing for the same customer meaning, even when their products differ. A market-entry decision may require incumbents, local operators, platforms and potential partners.

Define the decision, then establish a sensible boundary for the analysis. Too broad a competitor universe creates noise. Too narrow a universe can hide substitutes or new ways customers solve the same problem.

Track signals consistently

Choose observable dimensions that match the question: target audience and use cases; value proposition and promises; pricing structures and terms; product range; distribution and partnerships; trust signals and claims; public customer questions or friction. Record the source and date of every observation.

Use public, lawful information and do not present inferred motives as facts. A change in a website message is observable; the internal reason behind it usually is not.

Move from inventory to patterns

Look across the signals. Are competitors making the same promise? Are prices converging? Does every brand talk about features while few explain a use case? Are some audiences addressed repeatedly while others are largely ignored?

Patterns can generate hypotheses. They do not prove that an opportunity is commercially attractive. A space may be unclaimed because demand is weak, delivery is difficult or the economics do not work. Test the explanation rather than celebrating whitespace.

Map differences buyers care about

A competitor matrix should show meaningful differences, not just aesthetic ones. Choose axes that reflect buyer priorities, such as breadth versus specialization or convenience versus depth of support. Explain why the dimensions matter, and avoid arbitrary scores that look more precise than the evidence permits.

Turn the pattern into options

A useful brief sets out a small number of choices. A business might sharpen its audience, change the proof behind its promise, improve an overlooked customer journey, develop a partner route or deliberately avoid a price war.

  1. What evidence supports the opportunity?
  2. Which customer would value the difference?
  3. What capabilities or costs would be required?
  4. What could make the option fail?
  5. What is the smallest credible test?

Keep intelligence alive

Competitor analysis is not a one-off presentation that stays accurate forever. Establish a light monitoring rhythm around signals most likely to change a decision. Revisit assumptions when the market shifts, a new entrant appears or customer response differs from expectations.

The discipline is not to watch everything. It is to watch the right things, interpret them carefully and know when a new signal should trigger a decision review.
